# Mercury Propeller Selection Guide 2026

*Updated July 30, 2026: this guide was revised to correct propeller family specifications, materials, and gearcase fitment throughout.*

> **Quick answer:** Match the prop to your gearcase first, then your hull and load. Most aluminum fishing boats on Rice Lake start with a 3-blade aluminum prop. Command Thrust pontoons take larger props from the same catalogue as the 150 FourStroke. Confirm every choice with a wide-open-throttle test under normal load: WOT RPM should land near the top of Mercury's published range.

A propeller is the cheapest performance part on your boat and the one most people get wrong. The right prop gets you on plane faster, burns less fuel at cruise, and lets the motor live in the RPM range it was built for. The wrong prop lugs the engine, kills your hole-shot, or leaves speed sitting on the table.

Here's the thing most prop advice skips: you can't pick a prop from a chart alone. The chart gets you close. The water is the test. This guide gives you the chart, the families worth knowing, and the WOT test that confirms the answer.

---

## How Prop Pitch Works

Pitch is the theoretical distance, in inches, the prop would move forward in one full rotation if the water never slipped. A 19-pitch prop would travel 19 inches per revolution in perfect conditions. Real-world slip varies with hull, load, and speed.

The rule every dealer tunes by:

**Each inch of pitch is worth roughly 150 to 200 RPM at wide-open throttle.**

If the motor over-revs past its spec at WOT, the prop is under-pitched. Step up. If the motor can't reach its spec range, the prop is over-pitched and the engine is lugging under load. Step down.

The target is the **top of the manufacturer's WOT range** with the boat under normal load. That's Mercury's own guidance, and it's the part most charts get wrong. A motor propped to the top of its band under full load has headroom to stay in spec when you add a cooler, three friends, and a full tank. A motor propped to the bottom is one passenger away from lugging.

One catch before you start swapping: not every family offers every pitch, and some sizes jump in 2-inch steps. That changes the math, which is one of the reasons we check the actual catalogue before ordering.

## Diameter, Pitch, Blade Count, Rake, and Cup

Five things define how a prop behaves. Most guides only tell you about two.

**Diameter** is the width of the circle the blade tips draw. More diameter moves more water, which means more thrust at low speed and better grip with heavy loads. Diameter is largely set by your gearcase. [Command Thrust](https://www.mercuryrepower.ca/blog/mercury-command-thrust-real-talk-bigfoot-pontoon-v-hull) exists specifically to swing a bigger-diameter prop.

**Pitch** is the tuning dial. It's how you land WOT RPM in spec.

**Blade count** shapes hole-shot and handling. More blades grip better and plane easier. Fewer blades run faster on top.

**Rake** is the angle the blades sweep back from the hub. Higher rake lifts the bow, which helps a light performance hull run faster and hurts a heavy pontoon trying to push a load. This is why a bass prop and a pontoon prop are not interchangeable, even at the same pitch.

**Cup** is the small curled lip on the trailing edge of the blade. Cup improves bite, holds the prop hooked up in turns, and lets a motor trim higher before ventilating.

One more piece: the **hub**. Most current Mercury props use the Flo-Torq modular hub system, which means the hub kit is matched to your motor, not built into the prop. It's a small part with a big job (it's also the sacrificial piece that protects your gearcase in a strike), and it's part of every prop quote we do.

## Aluminum vs. Stainless Steel

**Aluminum** is the default for most fishing boats and lower-horsepower pontoons, and for any water where rocks, logs, and deadheads are part of life. Aluminum props cost less, flex before they fail, and are usually repairable. They give up some efficiency, and they wear faster.

**Stainless steel** is stiffer, holds its shape under load, and converts more power into forward motion. It's the default for higher-horsepower motors and performance hulls. The trade-off is cost, and the fact that a hard strike transfers more energy toward the hub and gearcase instead of bending a blade.

Worth knowing: Mercury's better stainless props (Enertia, Fury, Revolution 4 among them) are cast from Mercury's own X7 alloy, a proprietary stainless that Mercury rates as stronger and more durable than conventional stainless. That includes the SpitFire X7, which gets mistaken for an aluminum prop constantly because the original SpitFire is aluminum. The X7 is stainless. Different prop, different money, different performance.

As a rough planning number, expect a stainless prop to cost two to three times what a comparable aluminum prop does. Prop pricing moves with Mercury's price list, so we'll give you the current CAD number when you ask.

For Rice Lake and the Kawarthas, aluminum covers most recreational use. Stainless starts making sense on higher-horsepower rigs, performance hulls, and bigger pontoons where the efficiency gain pays for itself.

## 3-Blade vs. 4-Blade

**3-blade** props are the default for most aluminum fishing boats and general-purpose use. Best top-end for a given pitch, least drag at cruise, simplest tuning.

**4-blade** props improve hole-shot, low-speed control, and grip in turns, at a cost of roughly 1 to 3 mph on top. They're the usual answer for pontoons, tow boats, and any hull that struggles to plane.

The exception that proves the rule: Mercury's Enertia ECO is a 3-blade, but it's a big 16-inch-diameter 3-blade built for fuel-efficient cruising on high-horsepower rigs. Blade count is a design choice, not a category. Which is why the families below matter more than counting blades.

Three notes the internet keeps getting wrong:

**Command Thrust tops out at 115 HP.** CT is offered on specific mid-range motors (9.9 ProKicker through 115). A 200 HP tritoon motor doesn't have a CT option; it already has a big gearcase. If a chart shows "Command Thrust" beside a 200 HP motor, close the tab.

**The 90/115 Command Thrust shares its gearcase family with the 150 FourStroke.** That's the whole trick: same class of gearcase, bigger prop catalogue. A CT pontoon owner picks from the larger prop series, not a shorter list.

**Bass props don't belong on pontoons.** High-rake, high-pitch props like the Fury 4 are built for 200+ HP tournament hulls. On a tritoon they'd lug the engine hundreds of RPM below its floor. Family fit matters as much as blade count.

This table is a starting point, not a final answer. Load, elevation, and how your boat actually sits in the water all move the target. Which brings us to the test.

## The WOT RPM Test

Every prop decision gets confirmed at wide-open throttle under normal load. On a brand-new motor, finish [break-in](https://www.mercuryrepower.ca/blog/breaking-in-new-mercury-motor-guide) first. Then run the protocol.

1. **Load the boat the way you actually use it.** People aboard, gear loaded, fuel where it usually sits. An empty boat gives you a number you can't trust.
2. **Find safe, open water** with room to run straight for a minute.
3. **Settle into normal running trim.** Bow neither pinned down nor sky-high.
4. **Hold wide-open throttle until the RPM settles.** Watch the tach.
5. **Compare against Mercury's published WOT range for your exact motor model.** It's in your operator manual, and the band belongs to the model code, not the horsepower (more on that below).
6. **Apply the rule:**
   - WOT RPM below the range: over-pitched, engine is lugging. Drop pitch.
   - WOT RPM near the top of the range: correct prop.
   - WOT RPM over the range: under-pitched, engine over-revs. Add pitch.
7. **Re-test after any change.** One pitch step is 150-200 RPM. Check the family's actual pitch increments before assuming a 1-inch step exists.

If you bought the motor here, that check should have happened during the dealer water test. If the boat came to you used or re-rigged, get a WOT test done your first season. It's the cheapest insurance the driveline gets.

## Same Horsepower, Different RPM Band

This is the detail that catches people: **the WOT range belongs to the model, not the horsepower.** Three current 150-class Mercurys carry different bands. For example, the 150 FourStroke runs a 5000-5800 RPM band while the 150 Pro XS runs 5200-6000. Same displacement, different calibration, different target.

So never prop to "a 150." Prop to *your* 150, from the spec in *your* operator manual. When in doubt, the manual wins over every chart on the internet, including this one.

## Common Mercury Prop Families at HBW

The families you'll actually see on Ontario freshwater motors, and what they're for:

**Black Max.** Mercury's standard 3-blade aluminum prop. Ships on a huge range of fishing motors. Field-repairable and cheap to own. The default until you have a reason to leave it.

**SpitFire.** The 4-blade aluminum option for mid-range motors. Better hole-shot than a 3-blade aluminum for pontoons and loaded boats on a budget.

**SpitFire X7.** A 4-blade in Mercury's X7 stainless alloy, built for 75-115 HP motors on the standard gearcase (and 40-60 CT). The step-up prop for a hard-working aluminum boat or tow rig in that power range. Not a fit for the 90/115 Command Thrust gearcase, which takes the larger prop series below.

**Enertia.** 3-blade X7 stainless performance prop. Deep-Vs, performance fishing hulls, cruisers.

**Enertia ECO.** The 16-inch 3-blade built for cruise efficiency on higher-horsepower rigs, including big pontoons that spend their life at cruise rather than pulling tubes.

**Revolution 4.** Mercury's workhorse 4-blade stainless for the larger gearcases, including 90/115 Command Thrust and 150+ motors. Hole-shot, load-carrying, watersports grip. If you own a CT pontoon and want stainless, this is usually where the conversation starts.

**Vengeance.** The value 3-blade stainless for larger motors. A sensible step up from aluminum when the budget doesn't reach Enertia money.

**Fury and Fury 4.** X7 stainless tournament bass props. High rake, high pitch, built for 200+ HP performance hulls running Torque Master or Sport Master gearcases. Spectacular in their lane, wrong everywhere else.

**Bravo I FS.** The high-performance 4-blade that crossed over from racing. Performance tritoons and high-horsepower applications.

There are more families than this. These cover most of what we rig and service.

Before any prop leaves the counter, we confirm four things: the exact gearcase on your motor (model code, not horsepower), the part-number fitment for that gearcase, the hub kit your motor needs, and the pitch increments the family actually comes in. Fifteen minutes of checking beats owning a beautiful prop that doesn't fit. That's also why our prop advice comes with a WOT number to hit, not just a part to buy.

One example of why the hub check matters: on the gearcase family shared by the 150 FourStroke and the 90/115 Command Thrust, Mercury's application charts note that stainless props at 18 pitch and lower can produce clutch rattle without the right hub kit. ## Common mistakes

**Buying for a top speed you never use.** If the lake keeps you at 30 mph, prop for economy and cruise manners at 30, not for a brochure number.

**Skipping the WOT test after a new prop.** Recommendations get you close. The water is the test. No prop is confirmed until it's been run under your normal load.

**Running over-pitched to lower cruise RPM.** An over-pitched prop doesn't relax the engine, it loads it. Lugging below the WOT floor builds heat and wear. Let the motor rev where Mercury designed it to.

**Replacing a bent prop without asking what bent it.** A prop that found a rock may have passed the hit into the hub or gearcase. New prop on a damaged shaft is a short-lived fix.

**Propping around a hull or load problem.** A boat that rides heavy, holds water, or carries all its weight aft will under-perform with every prop until the real issue is fixed.

## FAQs

### How do I choose the right Mercury propeller for my boat?

Start with four variables: gearcase, hull type, normal load, and what you want the boat to do. Most aluminum fishing boats run a 3-blade aluminum prop on a standard gearcase. Command Thrust pontoons run larger props from the 150-class catalogue, usually a 4-blade stainless like the Revolution 4. Confirm with a wide-open-throttle test under normal load, targeting the top of Mercury's published RPM range.

### What is the rule for pitch and RPM on a Mercury outboard?

Each inch of pitch is worth roughly 150 to 200 RPM at wide-open throttle. Over-revving past spec means add pitch. Cannot reach spec means drop pitch. Note that some prop families only come in 2-inch increments, so a 1-inch step is not always available in the family you are running.

### Should my WOT RPM be in the middle or at the top of the range?

Near the top. Mercury's guidance is that engine RPM at wide-open throttle under normal load should land near the top of the published range. That leaves headroom so extra passengers and gear do not drag the motor below spec into lugging territory.

### Should I use a 3-blade or 4-blade Mercury prop?

3-blade gives the best top end and least drag, and it is the default for most fishing boats. 4-blade gives better hole-shot, low-speed control, and grip, at a cost of 1 to 3 mph up top, and it is the default for pontoons and tow boats. Blade count follows the job, not the other way around: Mercury's Enertia ECO is a 3-blade built specifically for high-horsepower cruise efficiency.

### Should I choose aluminum or stainless steel?

Aluminum for most fishing boats, lower-horsepower pontoons, and rocky or stump-filled water: cheaper, repairable, forgiving. Stainless for higher horsepower and performance hulls: stiffer blades, better efficiency, more speed. Expect stainless to cost roughly two to three times as much. Watch the names: the SpitFire is aluminum, but the SpitFire X7 is Mercury's X7 stainless alloy.

### Do Command Thrust gearcases need a different prop?

Yes, and it is good news. Command Thrust uses a larger gearcase, and the 90/115 CT shares its gearcase family with the 150 FourStroke, which opens up the larger prop catalogue: Revolution 4, Enertia ECO, and more. Fitment still comes down to the exact part number and hub kit, so confirm before ordering.

### How do I know if my prop is over-pitched or under-pitched?

Run the WOT test under normal load. Below the spec range: over-pitched, the engine is lugging under too much load. Above the range: under-pitched, the engine over-revs. Near the top of the range: correct. Each pitch step moves WOT RPM about 150 to 200.
